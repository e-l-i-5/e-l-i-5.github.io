The Planet That Could Float in a Bathtub

Astronomy, explained like you are five.

Imagine a bathtub the size of the Solar System. Fill it with water and lower the planets in, one by one. Mercury sinks. Earth sinks. Even mighty Jupiter sinks. But one planet would bob at the surface like a rubber duck: Saturn, the only planet in our Solar System less dense than water. That is the bathtub paradox that opens the video -- a giant 95 times heavier than Earth, yet light enough to float. How does a gas giant get away with it?

The secret is what Saturn is made of: mostly gas, wrapped around a core burning at about 11,700 degrees Celsius. All that gas spreads enormous mass across enormous space, so the average density stays low. But do not picture a calm balloon. Saturn's atmosphere whips up winds at 1,800 kilometers per hour -- bullet speed -- and the poles decorate the chaos with geometry: a hexagon storm spins at the north pole, a decagon at the south. Weather with straight edges, drawn by jet streams instead of pencils.

Then there is the treasure everyone knows: the rings. The video asks what they are actually made of and dates the debate -- are the rings ancient survivors, or young debris? Its answer leans young: the wreckage of a destroyed moon named Chrysalis, shredded into countless icy pieces. The tragic origin makes the jewelry: the brightest rings in the neighborhood may be the newest, the remains of a moon that no longer exists.

Saturn is also the Moon King of the Solar System, with 293 confirmed moons -- a whole miniature empire. Titan wears orange smog and pools liquid methane in lakes, a cycle of weather made of a different chemistry than Earth's. Enceladus hides a saltwater ocean under the ice and fires geysers of that ocean into space -- a small moon announcing its inner sea to any spacecraft that flies by. Two moons, two completely different ways to be a world.

Add the diamond rain said to fall deep inside, and Saturn earns its nickname, the jewel of the Solar System -- a planet that overflows with paradoxes. It floats like a toy but storms like a machine; it wears ancient-looking rings that may be young; it rules 293 moons while being light enough for a bathtub. Its sideways-leaning sibling Uranus has its own story on this site; Saturn's is the story of the floater -- proof that in planetary science, the heaviest-looking worlds often hide the strangest numbers.

The short version

  • Saturn is the only planet less dense than water -- 95 Earth masses that would float in a big enough bathtub.
  • Its weather is violent and geometric: 1,800 km/h winds, a north-pole hexagon and a south-pole decagon; the rings are likely young wreckage of the destroyed moon Chrysalis.
  • With 293 confirmed moons it is the Moon King: Titan pools liquid methane under orange smog, Enceladus sprays its saltwater ocean into space as geysers.

FAQ

Why would Saturn float in water?

Because its average density is lower than water's. Saturn is 95 times heavier than Earth, but most of that mass is gas spread over a huge volume, so the planet as a whole would bob like a rubber duck.

What are Saturn's rings made of?

Icy debris -- and possibly young debris: the video favors the idea that the rings are the wreckage of a destroyed moon called Chrysalis rather than ancient leftovers.

What is the hexagon on Saturn?

A hexagon-shaped storm spinning at the north pole, with a decagon counterpart at the south pole -- geometric weather produced by the planet's extreme jet streams, alongside 1,800 km/h winds.

What is special about Titan and Enceladus?

Titan, the largest of Saturn's 293 confirmed moons, has liquid methane lakes under orange smog; Enceladus hides a saltwater ocean beneath its ice and shoots geysers of it into space.
