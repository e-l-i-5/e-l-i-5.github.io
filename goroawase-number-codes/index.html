The Secret Code of Japanese Wordplay: Goroawase, Puns & Number Hacks

Tokyo's tallest tower, the Skytree, stands exactly 634 meters tall -- and that number was not an engineering accident. It was chosen to make a pun. In Japanese, 6-3-4 can be read as "mu-sa-shi," the old name of the region where the tower stands. One of the most advanced structures on Earth is secretly a joke, and it is not alone: the entire Japanese language works like a giant sandbox of hidden codes.

The trick is called goroawase, and it exploits one simple fact: in Japanese, numbers have several possible readings. The digit 2 can be "ni," the digit 9 can be "ku" -- and together they sound exactly like "niku," the word for meat. That is why the 29th of the month is unofficially "Meat Day" in Japan, a real holiday for carnivores. Stack more digits and the codes get longer: type 4-6-4-9 into a text message and a Japanese reader sees "yo-ro-shi-ku," a friendly phrase meaning roughly "nice to meet you" or "please treat me well." Even 11 becomes wordplay, since its reading sounds like "wan-wan" -- the Japanese version of a dog's bark.

The love of double meanings is ancient. Classical poets used kakekotoba, "pivot words" -- a single word placed so it could be read two ways at once, tying two lines of a poem together with one sound. Modern descendants survive as dajare, Japan's famous homophone puns, the kind of groan-worthy "dad jokes" built on two words that share a pronunciation. There are even number-based riddles whispered in classrooms, like the dark little equation 18782 + 18782 -- a joke better left undecoded at the dinner table.

Then there is the most surgical weapon in the wordplay arsenal: ginatayomi, the art of moving a tiny pause. Keep the pause in one place and a sentence says "baking bread." Slide it a syllable over, and the very same sounds collapse into "eating underpants." Nothing changes except an invisible sliver of silence -- and the meaning flips completely.

Here is the twist: this is not just silliness, it is a cultural operating system. Dates advertise products, phone numbers hide greetings, towers carry regional pride in their heights, and poets built centuries of layered verse on pivot words. Once you learn to read numbers as syllables, Japan stops being a country that occasionally makes puns and becomes a country where ordinary counting is already a secret language -- you just have to know which reading to pick.

The short version

  • Japanese numbers have multiple readings, so goroawase wordplay turns digits into messages: 29 sounds like "niku" (meat), 4649 spells out "yoroshiku" (nice to meet you), and 11 barks like a dog.
  • The habit is ancient: classical poets packed double meanings into single "pivot words" (kakekotoba), and modern dajare puns still trade on identical-sounding words.
  • Moving a tiny pause (ginatayomi) can flip "baking bread" into "eating underpants," and even Tokyo Skytree's height of 634 m was chosen to sound like "Musashi," the old name of the region.

FAQ

What is goroawase?

It is Japanese number wordplay. Because each digit has several possible pronunciations, a string of numbers can be re-read as a word or phrase -- 29 becomes "niku" (meat), and 4649 becomes "yoroshiku," a friendly greeting. It is used in dates, texting codes, and even building heights.

Why is the 29th of the month "Meat Day"?

The digit 2 is read "ni" and 9 is read "ku," so 29 sounds like "niku" -- the Japanese word for meat. Restaurants and meat lovers treat the 29th as an unofficial holiday built entirely on this pun.

What did Tokyo Skytree's designers do with wordplay?

They fixed the tower's height at exactly 634 meters because 6-3-4 can be read as "mu-sa-shi," the historical name of the region where the tower stands. The structure is not just an engineering landmark -- its height is a permanent, city-sized pun.
