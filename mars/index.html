Mars: The Secrets of Our Rust-Covered Neighbor

Mars — the Red Planet, explained like you're five.

The Red Next Door

Pack light for the strangest trip of your life. The sky above you glows pink, the ground under your boots is rust-red, and every step bounces: gravity here is only 38 percent of Earth's. You could leap like a superhero — on a world named after the god of war.

Mars is Earth's next-door neighbor, the fourth rock from the Sun, and it is covered in real rust — the same chemistry that turns an old bicycle orange. Iron-rich dust coats the entire planet, which is why telescopes see a red dot while landers stand in an orange desert. Up close, the dust even reveals surprise colors no telescope could ever catch.

And it is a land of extremes. Olympus Mons is a volcano three times taller than Mount Everest — on a planet smaller than ours. Valles Marineris is a mega-canyon stretching 4,000 kilometers, so wide and deep it makes the Grand Canyon look like a scratch. The air is thin and toxic — less than one percent of what you breathe at home — yet the weather still rages: dust storms howl at 160 km/h, and the snow that falls is made of frozen carbon dioxide, dry ice.

But the biggest secret of Mars is the water. Spacecraft have photographed dry lake beds and river valleys — evidence of ancient oceans on a world that now looks like a frozen junkyard. Where did all that water go? The short answer: the Sun stole it. Four billion years ago Mars had flowing rivers, a thick atmosphere and a magnetic shield generated by its hot core. Then the core cooled, the shield collapsed, and the solar wind began stripping the atmosphere away molecule by molecule, like cosmic billiard balls. Thin air cannot keep water warm and liquid, so the oceans froze or vanished.

They did not all leave, though. Massive reserves of water ice are still hiding on Mars today, locked away where the Sun cannot reach. That ice is why scientists keep asking one extra question: if Mars had lakes, rivers and maybe oceans for millions of years — did anything ever live in them? The red planet keeps its answer buried under the rust, waiting for us to dig.

FAQ

Why is Mars red?

Mars is covered in real iron rust — the same chemistry that turns an old bicycle orange. Iron-rich dust coats the whole planet, so telescopes see a red dot and landers see an orange desert.

Could you really jump like a superhero on Mars?

Yes — surface gravity on Mars is only 38% of Earth's. On a world smaller than ours, every step bounces and your body would move like a superhero in a movie.

What happened to the water on Mars?

Four billion years ago Mars had flowing rivers and a thick atmosphere protected by a magnetic shield. When the core cooled and the shield collapsed, the solar wind stripped the atmosphere away molecule by molecule — and without thick air, the oceans froze or vanished. Massive reserves of water ice are still hiding on Mars today.

How big are Olympus Mons and Valles Marineris?

Olympus Mons is a volcano three times taller than Mount Everest. Valles Marineris is a mega-canyon stretching about 4,000 kilometers — so wide and deep it makes the Grand Canyon look like a scratch.
