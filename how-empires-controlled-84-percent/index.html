How Empires Controlled 84 Percent of the World

Taking over most of the planet, as the empires had done by 1914, was the easy-looking part. Holding it with a few thousand officials per colony, year after year, without televisions, phones or airports — that is the genuinely strange part. Imagine a faraway boss who has never seen your street moving into the neighborhood, rewriting all the house rules from a distance, taking a cut of everyone's lunch, and acting as if his way of life is the only correct one. How did something so absurd actually work? The answer is a toolbox of control, and every tool had a purpose.

The first tool was the tax that had to be paid in money. Before colonization, many communities lived partly outside the cash economy: they farmed, traded, and owed duties to local rulers in crops or labor. The empire demanded taxes in its own currency, which meant every family suddenly needed cash — and the only reliable way to get cash was to work for the colonizers: in their mines, on their plantations, building their railways. A piece of paper about tax rates quietly turned millions of independent farmers into a cheap workforce. Nobody forced anybody at gunpoint every morning; the arithmetic did it.

The second tool was the cash crop. Colonies were steered to grow what the empire wanted — cotton in India, rubber in the Congo, sugar in the Caribbean, tea in Ceylon, cocoa in West Africa — rather than the food people ate. When the world price of that crop crashed, farmers who could no longer eat their own fields starved. Famines in colonial India happened while trains carrying grain to ports ran on schedule. The colony was not run for the people inside it; it was run as a machine that turned local land and labor into exports.

The third tool was indirect rule: do not govern everybody yourself — govern the few who govern everybody. Empires kept sultans, chiefs and kings in place, paid from imperial budgets, promoted if loyal and removed if not. The British perfected the method in places like Nigeria; the French preferred a more direct style that tried to turn locals into little Parisians through schools and law. Either way, the message to ordinary people was the same: even your own leader now answers to a faraway office, and resistance means replacing him too.

The fourth tool was culture. Colonial schools taught the empire's language, history and map of the world, so that the smartest children of the colony could read, calculate, obey — and graduate believing the boss's way of living was simply the modern way to be human. Local languages were pushed out of offices and courts; local names of streets, mountains and cities were replaced. Some of this survives to this day in which languages billions of people still conduct business in, and in whose heroes appear in whose schoolbooks.

Control also had a hard edge, and honesty requires naming it. Rebellions were crushed with overwhelming force; in German East Africa, the suppression of the Maji Maji rising around 1907 left hundreds of thousands dead, many by engineered famine. In the Congo under King Leopold's personal rule, the rubber quota system mutilated and murdered on a scale that shocked the world into the first great human-rights protest movements. Prisons, pass laws and hostage villages were standard administration, not accidents.

And yet the toolbox had a design flaw: it trained the very people who would end it. Colonial schools produced lawyers and journalists who read the empire's own books about rights and law — and asked why the books stopped at the border. Colonies raised local regiments that fought in the empire's world wars and came home asking why the freedom they had fought for did not include their own streets. After 1945 the weakened empires faced strikes, parties, newspapers and uprisings at every point at once. India left in 1947, Ghana in 1957, and in 1960 alone seventeen African countries became independent. Within a generation, the map of the 84 percent had been rolled back to a few scattered islands — kept alive only by the questions it left behind, which is what the faraway boss always forgets: the neighborhood eventually reads the rules back to him.

The short version

  • Tax demanded in cash created forced labor by arithmetic; cash crops fed the empire, not the colony.
  • Indirect rule governed through local chiefs; schools taught the imperial worldview; rebellions were crushed brutally (Maji Maji, Congo).
  • The flaw: empire-trained lawyers and veteran regiments turned its own ideals against it — India 1947, Ghana 1957, 17 African states in 1960.

FAQ

Why did empires prefer indirect rule?

It was cheaper and quieter. Governing through existing chiefs used familiar authority, needed few European staff, and gave victims a local face to blame — while the faraway office kept the real power. If the chief became inconvenient, he was replaced; the system itself stayed above local politics.

Did colonies ever profit the people living in them?

Some infrastructure appeared — railways, ports, hospitals, schools — but it was planned around extraction: lines ran from plantations and mines to ports, not between local towns. Most profit flowed outward. Economists still measure colonial-era drains of wealth from India, and the gap between the export machine and local living standards is the honest answer.

How did colonization end so quickly after 1945?

Two world wars exhausted the empires, and colonized soldiers, workers and thinkers returned with skills, networks and demands. The United States and the Soviet Union, the new superpowers, both opposed old-style colonialism for their own reasons. With debt at home and resistance everywhere, holding the map became more expensive than letting it go — so most of it went between 1945 and 1975.
