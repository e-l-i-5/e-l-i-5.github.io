How an Ancient Wooden Calculator Crushed Modern Tech 4 to 1

Can a simple wooden tray with sliding beads beat an electric calculator? In Tokyo in 1946, the answer was a public humiliation for the machine age: a Japanese soroban master named Kiyoshi Matsuzaki faced off against a US Army electric calculator — and won 4 to 1. To understand how wood beat wires, you have to look at the soroban not as a counting relic but as a hand-operated computer with remarkably good design.

The soroban arrived in Japan from China's suanpan and then got ruthlessly streamlined. Its anatomy is minimalist: a reckoning bar divides the frame, and each column holds exactly five beads — one upper bead called Go-dama, worth 5, and four lower beads called Ichi-dama, worth 1 each. That is the entire hardware. Earlier designs carried extra, mostly idle beads; Japanese mathematicians cut them away, leaving the leanest configuration that still counts everything from 0 to 9 per column.

The "software" is the clever part: every bead is a binary on/off switch. A bead is either pressed to the bar (on) or away from it (off) — nothing in between. Because the upper bead means 5 and the lower beads mean 1, each column encodes digits in what mathematicians call bi-quinary coded decimal: a two-tier system where you read one "five-bead" plus a count of ones. Dialing a number like 8,036 is just setting switches: one Go-dama down plus three Ichi-dama in the thousands column, nothing in the hundreds, three Ichi-dama in the tens, and one Go-dama plus one Ichi-dama in the units. Reading the tray IS reading the number — no parsing, no display, just pattern recognition.

And that is the secret of the 1946 victory. Electric calculators of the era required the operator to translate numbers into keystrokes and wait for the machine; the soroban operator's fingers move on reflex, and the arithmetic becomes lightning-fast pattern recognition. The beads live in the fingers, not on a screen. The operator never "thinks" about 8 + 5 — the hand already knows the shape of the answer.

The training goes one step further with Anzan: after enough practice, students install the soroban directly into the brain. They visualize the tray, and their mental fingers flick the mental beads — calculating entirely in the mind, often faster than people can type the problem. Mental athletes equipped with Anzan add multi-digit columns faster than an observer can read the numbers aloud.

Why does the soroban still matter in the smartphone era? Because it never competed on raw computing power — it competes on the human interface. Beads are tactile, silent, unbreakable by dead batteries, and they teach number sense the way no screen does. The machine age took a step back in Tokyo in 1946 for a good reason: the best computer in the room was the one the operator could feel.

FAQ

What does bi-quinary coded decimal mean?

It is a two-tier way of encoding each digit: one part for the fives (the single upper bead) and one part for the ones (the four lower beads). Every column therefore represents 0–9 with just five beads, and reading the bead pattern is the same as reading the number.

How did the soroban differ from the Chinese suanpan?

The Japanese inherited the Chinese suanpan and stripped it down. Extra beads that added little were removed, leaving the strict 1-upper/4-lower layout — a deliberate minimalist redesign that made the instrument faster to read and quicker to flick.

Did the soroban really beat an electric calculator?

Yes — in a 1946 Tokyo contest, soroban master Kiyoshi Matsuzaki competed against a US Army electric calculator operated by a trained soldier and won 4 to 1 across the challenges. The abacus operator's reflexes and instant pattern recognition outran the machine's slower input process.

What is Anzan?

Anzan is mental calculation built on a virtual soroban: after years of physical practice, the user imagines the frame and moves imaginary beads, computing entirely in the mind. Skilled Anzan users add long columns of numbers faster than the digits can be spoken aloud.
