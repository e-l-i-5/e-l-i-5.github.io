The Explainer: Sodium

Nutrition & biochemistry, explained like you are five.

Take a grain of salt — seriously, take one. Inside that tiny crystal sits an atom your body cannot live without, and the same atom that doctors keep warning you about. Sodium is the perfect example of a simple truth in nutrition: the difference between essential and harmful is usually just a matter of dose.

Start with what sodium actually does. Your body runs on electricity, and sodium is the charge carrier. Every thought you have, every heartbeat, every command that travels from brain to finger — all of it rides on sodium atoms swapping places across cell membranes, tiny batteries firing in sequence. Nerves are sodium wires; muscles are sodium switches. Water follows sodium too: the mineral controls how much fluid stays inside your blood vessels and cells, which is why it quietly manages your blood pressure every second of the day.

Your body guards this system obsessively. Sensors constantly check sodium levels, and your kidneys fine-tune the balance, keeping the concentration in your blood almost eerily stable — it barely moves even when you eat a salty meal or sweat through a workout. When sodium runs low, you crave it; when it runs high, you get thirsty, and the extra water dilutes it back down.

The trouble starts with scale. For most of human history, salt was rare and precious — whole cities and trade routes were built on it, and soldiers were sometimes paid in it (the word "salary" carries that memory). Our bodies learned to hold onto every atom. Then, within a single century, salt became cheap and everywhere: processed foods, snacks, restaurant meals, bread. The stone-age safeguard now works against us, holding onto more sodium than our ancestors ever saw. Chronic excess nudges blood pressure upward, and high blood pressure quietly damages the heart, kidneys, and blood vessels for years before anyone notices.

The fix is not fear — it is balance. You need sodium every day; the question is how much, and most modern diets overshoot by a wide margin, with the majority hidden inside packaged food long before the salt shaker appears. Read labels, cook more from raw ingredients, and let the crystal go back to being what it was for your ancestors: a rare, precious gift — not a daily flood.

The short version

  • Sodium is the charge carrier of your body: nerves, muscles, and fluid balance all run on it — life without it is impossible.
  • Your kidneys keep blood sodium almost perfectly stable, craving salt when you are low and thirsting to dilute you when you are high.
  • Modern diets hide far more sodium than our salt-scarce ancestors ever faced — chronic excess raises blood pressure, and most of it comes from packaged food, not the shaker.

FAQ

Why does the body need sodium at all?

Nerve signals and muscle contractions depend on sodium atoms moving across cell membranes, and sodium also controls how much water stays in your blood and cells. Without it, neither your brain nor your heart could send a signal.

If sodium is essential, why do doctors warn about salt?

Because dose decides. The body needs a small, steady amount and holds onto it efficiently — a survival trait from times when salt was rare. Modern processed foods deliver several times more than needed, and chronic excess pushes blood pressure up.

Where does most dietary sodium come from?

Not from the salt shaker — the majority is hidden in processed and restaurant foods: bread, snacks, sauces, canned goods, and ready meals. That is why label reading matters more than skipping the shaker.

What is a sensible way to manage sodium?

Cook from raw ingredients when you can, check labels for sodium per serving, and trust your kidneys to handle normal variation. Balance, not elimination, is the goal — sodium is essential, just not in flood quantities.
