The Thanksgiving Food Coma Myth: It's NOT the Turkey! (Tryptophan Hack)

The plates are cleared, the couch is calling, and someone at the table says it with total confidence: "Well, you know, the turkey is full of tryptophan." Everyone nods, and the holiday nap begins. There is just one problem with this beloved family tradition — the science says you have been blaming the wrong dish all along.

Start with the bird itself. Turkey actually has a completely average amount of tryptophan compared to other meats. Nothing about the star of the table is special; its tryptophan levels are ordinary. And here is the deeper catch: even the tryptophan already floating in your bloodstream cannot simply walk into your brain.

Between your blood and your brain stands a strict gatekeeper called the blood-brain barrier. Tryptophan must cross it to have any effect, but on a normal day it gets heavily outcompeted — blocked by other, bulkier amino acids that crowd the same gate. Tryptophan is outnumbered and left waiting at the door.

Then comes the mountain of carbohydrates. Mashed potatoes, stuffing, rolls — when you pile your plate high with them, your body responds with a massive flood of insulin. And insulin acts like a traffic cop with a single instruction: clear the road. It pulls all of those competing amino acids out of the bloodstream and packs them into your muscles.

Suddenly the highway into the brain is empty. With the road completely cleared, tryptophan finally speeds through the gate — and once inside, it converts directly into melatonin, the hormone that knocks you out. The drowsiness that feels like the turkey's fault is really a chain reaction that started with the side dishes.

So the myth quietly dies at the dinner table. It was not the turkey that put you to sleep — it was that giant mountain of carbs. Scientists even have a formal name for this post-meal sleepiness: postprandial somnolence. The turkey keeps collecting the blame, and the potatoes keep getting away with it.

Maybe this year, when the usual speech about sleepy turkey starts again, you can smile and pass the truth along with the gravy: the bird is innocent. Watch who reaches for the seconds of stuffing instead — that is the real suspect.

FAQ

Does turkey really contain extra tryptophan?

No. Turkey has a completely average amount of tryptophan compared to other meats, so the bird does not deserve its sleepy reputation.

Why doesn't tryptophan simply reach the brain?

It has to cross the strict blood-brain barrier, and normally it gets heavily outcompeted there — other, bulkier amino acids block it and keep it waiting at the gate.

So what actually causes the food coma?

The carbs. A big serving of mashed potatoes, stuffing and rolls triggers a massive release of insulin; insulin acts like a traffic cop, pulling the competing amino acids out of the bloodstream and into your muscles, which clears the way for tryptophan.

What happens when tryptophan finally reaches the brain?

With the highway cleared, tryptophan speeds in and converts directly into melatonin — the hormone that makes you sleepy. The formal name for this post-meal drowsiness is postprandial somnolence.
