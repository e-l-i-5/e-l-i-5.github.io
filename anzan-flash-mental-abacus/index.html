The Invisible Calculator: How to Do Math Faster Than a Computer

Memory & learning, explained like you are five.

Can the human brain really calculate faster than a smartphone? On its face, the question sounds absurd — silicon versus neurons. But the mental abacus technique called Anzan turns the question serious: with training, human addition reaches speeds that look like glitches in the footage.

The proof is on the record. Flash Anzan champion Takeo Sasano added fifteen three-digit numbers in just 1.7 seconds — numbers flashed too fast for ordinary reading, yet summed effortlessly in his head. No fingers, no device, no visible effort. Just a mind running an invisible calculator.

Here is the trick behind the magic: Anzan practitioners learn the soroban — the Japanese abacus — so thoroughly that the physical tool disappears. Beginners slide real beads on a real frame; advanced students slide imaginary beads on an imaginary frame. The arithmetic stops being number-crunching and becomes bead-moving — and because the beads live in imagination, the calculator is invisible, portable, and always on.

The neuroscience confirms something remarkable is happening. EEG brain scans of experts show right-hemisphere visual processing lighting up — the region used for seeing and spatial imagery, not the usual left-side math circuits. Practitioners are not "computing" in the textbook sense; they are watching an abacus that only they can see, and reading the answer off it. Numbers have become mental pictures, and pictures process fast.

The method's gifts spill beyond arithmetic. The same training builds deep focus — you cannot visualize beads while distracted — and strengthens memory, with practitioners extending the skill to feats like root extraction. Math becomes the doorway; the real upgrades are attention and imagery.

The video ends with a question for everyone, expert or beginner: what invisible machine would you build? The mental abacus proves the blueprint exists — train one mental tool until it becomes pictures, and the speed limit of "thinking" quietly moves.

FAQ

What is Anzan?

The mental abacus technique: after mastering the physical soroban, you perform calculations entirely in your head by visualizing the beads moving. The abacus becomes an invisible calculator you carry everywhere.

How fast can it get?

Champion-level Flash Anzan is the record: Takeo Sasano summed fifteen three-digit numbers flashed in 1.7 seconds — faster than most people can simply read the numbers aloud.

What do brain scans reveal?

EEG studies show experts solving problems with right-hemisphere visual processing — the imagery side of the brain — rather than the usual left-side arithmetic regions. They are literally seeing the abacus, not calculating abstractly.

Does it help with anything besides math?

Yes: the intense visualization builds deep focus and stronger memory, and trained practitioners extend the skill to advanced feats like root extraction. The calculator is the entry point; attention and imagery are the prizes.
