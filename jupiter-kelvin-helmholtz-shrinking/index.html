Why Jupiter Is Actually Shrinking Every Year

Most planets are mirrors: they soak up the Sun's light and glow it back, giving out no more heat than they receive. Jupiter breaks the rule. The giant emits about twice as much heat as it absorbs from the Sun, radiating energy it has no obvious source for. A planet is not supposed to have its own furnace -- stars do that. So Jupiter sits in an uncomfortable middle: too small to ignite, yet glowing from within like something still trying.

The answer to the paradox is called the Kelvin-Helmholtz mechanism, and its logic is almost violent in its simplicity: Jupiter never stopped collapsing. Forming a planet means falling -- trillions of tons of gas falling inward under its own gravity -- and Jupiter, the video explains, is still falling into itself. All that infalling mass squeezes the planet, and squeezing generates heat; gravitational energy is converted directly into the warmth that leaks out into space. The collapse is slow enough that nobody would notice it happening, but the bill is being paid in full, every second, as raw heat.

How slow is the collapse? About one millimeter per year. Jupiter is actively shrinking -- a planet very quietly tightening its own belt, year after year, with no finish line in sight. Scaled to the largest body in the Solar System, a millimeter sounds like nothing, but the point is not the size: it is that the process never switched off. Formation did not end billions of years ago and leave a finished object behind. It is still running, and the shrinking is the receipt.

That slow, endless squeeze is not just a curiosity -- it is the engine behind Jupiter's violent mega-storms. The heat rising from within churns the atmosphere from below, feeding the turbulence that makes the giant's weather the most extreme in the neighborhood. The storms are not leftovers of an ancient accident; they are the visible exhaust of a planet still converting its own gravity into weather.

Which reframes the biggest planet entirely. Jupiter looks like a finished monument, fixed and permanent, the king of the Solar System. It is closer to a construction site that never closed -- still settling, still compressing, still paying for its own existence in heat and storms. Every year it is a millimeter smaller, and every year the engine underneath keeps the clouds boiling. The king is not finished. He is still being built.

FAQ

How can Jupiter give out more heat than it gets from the Sun?

Because the extra heat does not come from outside. Through the Kelvin-Helmholtz mechanism, Jupiter converts gravitational energy into heat as it keeps compressing under its own gravity -- the planet never stopped collapsing, so it keeps radiating more than it receives.

Is Jupiter actually getting smaller?

Yes, by roughly one millimeter per year. That is the ongoing collapse made visible: the planet is still settling under its own weight, which is exactly what generates the internal heat.

What do Jupiter's storms have to do with any of this?

The heat released by gravitational compression churns the atmosphere from within and fuels Jupiter's violent mega-storms. The weather is the visible exhaust of a planet that is still converting gravity into heat.
