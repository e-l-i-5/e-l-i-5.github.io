The Secret Superpower Your Brain Uses to Read Minds (Pragmatics)

Linguistics, explained like you are five.

Picture this: someone at dinner asks, "Can you pass the salt?" and you simply reply, "Yes," and keep eating. Technically, you answered the question perfectly. Everyone at the table will still think you have lost your mind. Why? Because your brain never treats words as a dictionary lookup. It hunts for intent &mdash; and that mind-reading trick has a name: pragmatics.

Linguists separate two layers of meaning. Semantics is what words literally say: "Can you pass the salt?" is a question about your ability to move a shaker. Pragmatics is what people actually mean: it is a polite request, and everyone decodes it in a fraction of a second without noticing. Meaning lives in the context, not in the letters.

Context does absurd amounts of work. Take three small words &mdash; "green light." In a car, it means go. At a party, it means the plan is approved. From a boss, it means start the project. Same words, completely different instructions &mdash; because the room, not the sentence, carries the message. Words like "I," "here," and "now" are even stranger: they have no fixed meaning at all until a person says them at a moment. "I" is a different human every time it is spoken.

Philosopher Paul Grice noticed that conversation runs on a hidden rulebook: people assume each other are truthful, informative, relevant, and clear. The magic happens when someone deliberately breaks a rule &mdash; that break is what we hear as sarcasm or irony. Say "Nice weather" during a downpour and nobody is confused about the rain; they hear the mockery precisely because the rule was violated on purpose. As Wittgenstein put it, meaning is use.

Philosopher J. L. Austin went one step further: some sentences do not describe reality &mdash; they change it. Saying "I apologize," "I promise," or "Meeting adjourned" performs an act, like a spell cast with plain words. No physical object moves, yet the social world is different after the sentence than before it.

This is why computers struggle with jokes. An AI can store every dictionary meaning, but the invisible math our brains do in milliseconds &mdash; tracking who said what, to whom, in which room &mdash; is exactly what makes humans such effortless mind readers.

The short version

  • Semantics is what words literally say; pragmatics is what the speaker actually means &mdash; and brains compute the second one automatically.
  • Context can rewrite a sentence completely: "green light" means go, approval, or permission depending on the room.
  • Breaking conversational rules on purpose is how sarcasm works &mdash; and phrases like "I apologize" actually change reality.

FAQ

What is the difference between semantics and pragmatics?

Semantics is the literal, dictionary meaning of words and sentences. Pragmatics is meaning in context &mdash; what the speaker intends and what the listener infers. "Can you pass the salt?" semantically asks about ability; pragmatically it is a request.

What is Grice's rulebook?

Philosopher Paul Grice described four unwritten rules of conversation: be truthful, be informative, be relevant, and be clear. Communication runs smoothly when both sides follow them &mdash; and when one side breaks a rule on purpose, we usually hear irony or sarcasm.

Why is it hard for computers to understand jokes?

Jokes depend on unspoken context: the situation, the relationship, the shared expectations. A machine can parse every word correctly and still miss the point, because the punchline lives in the gap between what was said and what was meant.

What does "meaning is use" mean?

Ludwig Wittgenstein's idea that a word's meaning is not a fixed definition but the way people actually use it. That is why the same phrase can politely request salt in one room and sound absurd in another.
