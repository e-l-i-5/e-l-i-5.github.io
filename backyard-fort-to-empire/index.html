From a Tiny Fort to One-Sixth of the Earth: Russia's Growth

Imagine building a tiny treehouse with your friends, and then watching it grow until it covers your whole town, your whole country and finally one eighth of the planet. That is, roughly, the thousand-year story of Russia, and it is easier to follow if you treat it as a map that keeps growing chapter by chapter.

Chapter one is the first fort. Around 862 the eastern Slavs and their Scandinavian rulers built Kievan Rus, a loose family of river towns. It grew rich on trade, then fell apart under Mongol blows in the 1200s. The survivors huddled around one modest town in the forest, Moscow, which spent the next two and a half centuries quietly collecting land: marriages, inheritances, purchases and small wars, one neighbor at a time.

Chapter two is the playground boss. In 1547 Ivan the Terrible crowned himself the first Tsar, broke the independent power of the nobles, created a permanent army and turned the country outward. The most astonishing part of his reign was not fought in Europe at all: bands of Cossacks and adventurers walked east through Siberia, fort by fort, river by river, until the map reached the Pacific Ocean.

Chapter three is the imperial explosion. Peter the Great built a fleet, beat Sweden and proclaimed the Russian Empire in 1721. The population boomed from millions into tens of millions, then over a hundred million, as Poland, Finland, the Caucasus and Central Asia were added. By the eve of the First World War the empire was the largest contiguous land mass on Earth.

Chapter four is the Soviet redrawing, when the empire collapsed and was rebuilt as a federation of republics under one party, still immense, still spanning eleven time zones. And chapter five is 1991, when that giant, in one year, peacefully split into fifteen separate countries.

The lesson of the growing map is that size is not the same as strength. The treehouse covered the town, but the bigger it grew, the more joints and seams it had. A country that spans a sixth of the planet must hold together communities that see the world in different languages, and that has always been the hardest construction job of all.

The short version

  • Kievan Rus formed around 862 as a family of river-trading towns and fell to Mongol invasion in the 1200s.
  • Moscow grew by inheritance, purchase and conquest for 250 years before becoming the center of a state.
  • Ivan the Terrible crowned himself first Tsar in 1547; Siberia was crossed to the Pacific within a century.
  • Peter the Great proclaimed the Empire in 1721; the map then absorbed the Baltics, Poland, the Caucasus and Central Asia.
  • In 1991 the Soviet Union split into fifteen countries, the largest peaceful division of territory in modern history.

FAQ

How did Moscow become the center of Russia?

After the Mongols destroyed Kievan Rus, Moscow spent centuries quietly collecting territory through inheritance, purchase, marriage and small wars. Its princes also won the right to collect tribute for the Mongols, which gave them money and power over rival towns long before Moscow was officially the capital.

How fast did Russia cross Siberia?

Astonishingly fast. Cossack explorers crossed the Urals in the 1580s, and by the middle of the 1600s Russian forts had reached the Pacific Ocean. The trick was rivers: Siberia's great rivers form a chain that carries travelers from one end of the continent to the other.

Was the Russian Empire the biggest country ever?

The Russian Empire and its Soviet successor covered about a sixth of the planet's land, the largest contiguous land empire in history. Only the British Empire held more total territory, but it was scattered across oceans, while Russia's lands touched each other.

What happened in 1991?

The Soviet Union dissolved into fifteen independent countries. Unlike the collapse of 1917, most borders changed by agreement rather than war, which is why the breakup is called the largest peaceful division of territory in modern history.
