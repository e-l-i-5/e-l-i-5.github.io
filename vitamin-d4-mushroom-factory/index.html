The Secret Sunshine Vitamin Humans Can't Make (Vitamin D4)

Nutrition & biochemistry, explained like you are five.

You have heard of Vitamin D2 and D3 — but there is a third, ultra-rare sibling hiding in the forest: Vitamin D4. Humans forge Vitamin D3 in their own skin using direct sunlight, a neat biological trick. Wild forest mushrooms, however, run their own microscopic chemical factories to craft a rare, custom-built nutrient that no human cell can produce: 22-Dihydroergocalciferol, better known as Vitamin D4. How does a fungus manage chemistry that our bodies never learned?

Every vitamin factory needs a starting chemical puzzle piece — a sterol template that gets transformed into the final vitamin. For most fungi, that template is Ergosterol, the standard building block that sunlight converts into ordinary Vitamin D2. This is the mass-production path of the mushroom world: same template, same familiar product, in mushrooms from the grocery store shelf to the forest floor.

But certain wild mushrooms run a rarer production line. Instead of reaching for the standard Ergosterol, they grab an unusual building block called 22,23-dihydroergosterol — a subtle variant of the template, one step off the beaten chemical path. It is the difference between a factory making the everyday model and a workshop building a limited edition: same machinery, rarer ingredient, unique result.

The magic step is the catalyst: ultraviolet light. When UV rays from the sun strike this rare molecule, its structure snaps open and undergoes a photochemical rearrangement — the atoms shuffle into a brand-new shape. Out of that rearranged structure comes 22-dihydroergocalciferol: Vitamin D4, nature's custom-built sunshine nutrient, forged in the shade of the forest where wild mushrooms grow. Notice the beautiful irony — just like human skin, the mushrooms use sunlight to build a D-vitamin, but their workshop and their template are their own.

This little-known process says something bigger about nutrition: "vitamin D" is not one substance but a family of sunlight-forged molecules, each born from a different template in a different organism. We get our D3 from our own skin; mushrooms make D2 — and, in rare corners of the wild, D4 — from theirs. The forest keeps microscopic factories running quietly under our feet, producing nutrients with chemistry we cannot copy inside our bodies — only borrow from nature.

FAQ

What is Vitamin D4?

Vitamin D4 is 22-dihydroergocalciferol — an ultra-rare member of the vitamin D family. Unlike D2 and D3, it is not something humans produce or commonly consume; it is crafted by certain wild mushrooms under sunlight.

How do humans and mushrooms differ in making D-vitamins?

Humans forge Vitamin D3 directly in their skin using sunlight. Fungi are the opposite: they start from a sterol template inside their tissue and let UV light transform it — most make D2, and rare wild mushrooms make D4.

What is the role of 22,23-dihydroergosterol?

It is the rare starting template. While most fungi use the standard Ergosterol to produce Vitamin D2, certain wild mushrooms grab this uncommon building block instead — and it is precisely what makes their final product Vitamin D4 rather than D2.

How does sunlight create Vitamin D4 in mushrooms?

Ultraviolet rays hit the rare sterol template and snap its structure open, triggering a photochemical rearrangement. The atoms reshuffle into a new molecule — 22-dihydroergocalciferol, the custom-built sunshine vitamin hidden in the forest shade.
