From Ancient Rome to Mars: The Long Story of Colonization

Picture a playground sandbox. Several kids are already in it, building roads and hills, following games that have been running for years. A new kid walks up, does not join their game, and instead builds one enormous sandcastle right in the middle — and then announces that the whole sandbox is his, because he has the biggest castle and the loudest voice. Now stretch that image over three thousand years, from bronze-age ships to rockets, and you have the full story of colonization.

The playbook is ancient. Phoenician sailors, the master traders of the Mediterranean, planted trading posts on far coasts almost three thousand years ago; one of their posts grew into Carthage, which became an empire that fought Rome for survival. The Greeks were champions of the sandbox too: their crowded cities sent ships east and west, and colonies like Syracuse in Sicily and Byzantium — the future Istanbul — grew from those landings. Colonization was already old when Rome turned it into an assembly line.

Rome even gave the thing its name. The Latin word colonia meant a settlement planted by the state, and the Romans built dozens of them. Retired soldiers — veterans of twenty-five years of service — were granted farmland in conquered provinces, where they kept Roman law, Roman roads and Roman loyalty alive far from Rome itself. The city of Cologne in Germany still carries the name: the Romans called it Colonia. A colony was a copy of home, dropped onto somebody else's yard, with walls included.

Then the ocean era began. In 1492 Columbus sailed west, and within a generation Spanish and Portuguese ships had grabbed whole continents. The sandbox got so big that two kingdoms had to invent a rule for sharing it: the Treaty of Tordesillas in 1494 drew a line on the map and gave everything on one side to Spain and everything on the other to Portugal — without asking a single person who actually lived there. Two kids, one line in the sand, millions of people not invited to the negotiation. That treaty is the clearest example in history of how colonization worked as paperwork first and swords second.

The next four centuries followed the sandcastle pattern: arrive, build the biggest structure, claim the surroundings. North and South America, big parts of Asia, Africa and Oceania were carved into colonies. Then, in the twentieth century, the colonized world pushed back, empire after empire collapsed, and by the 1970s the classic sandbox was mostly gone — dozens of new countries raised their own flags over the castles that had been built on top of them.

Which brings the story to a strange modern twist: space. When the rocket age arrived, the grown-ups of the world remembered the sandbox and decided to write the rules early. The Outer Space Treaty of 1967 says no country may claim the Moon, Mars or any part of space as its own; the Antarctica Treaty of 1959 does the same for the South Pole, reserving it for science. For the first time in three thousand years, the kids agreed before building that the sandbox belongs to no single sandcastle. When people talk today about "colonizing Mars," they are therefore re-litigating the oldest argument in the yard: will the first settlers treat the new world as shared ground with common rules, or will somebody's giant sandcastle simply appear in the middle and start collecting rent?

History's lesson is not that colonization is always the same — trading posts, veteran farms, plantation islands and gold rushes worked differently — but that the move repeats: the strong arrive where others already live, and the rules get rewritten to match the newcomers. The sandbox question is a thousand years older than any rocket, and Mars will not be the first time it has been asked. It will only be the first time it has been answered in advance.

The short version

  • Colonization is 3,000 years old: Phoenician posts, Greek Syracuse and Byzantium, and Rome's veteran farms (colonia — Cologne keeps the name).
  • The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) split "new" continents between Spain and Portugal with one line on the map.
  • Space pre-wrote the rules: the 1967 Outer Space Treaty bans owning the Moon or Mars — the first treaty before the sandcastle.

FAQ

Why did Rome plant colonies instead of just conquering?

Conquest costs money every year; a colony pays rent, grows food and guards itself. Veteran settlements kept retired soldiers loyal, spread Roman law and language, and held a province with farmers instead of a permanent army. A colony was conquest converted into maintenance.

What was the Treaty of Tordesillas?

A 1494 agreement in which Spain and Portugal drew a north-south line across the world map: new lands west of it would be Spain's, east of it Portugal's. It is famous for dividing continents between two kingdoms that had no idea what — or who — was actually there.

Can anyone legally own Mars?

Under the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, no country may claim sovereignty over celestial bodies, so Mars cannot become anyone's national territory. Companies and individuals fall into a gray zone the treaty never imagined — which is why scientists keep saying the old sandbox arguments are about to have a sequel.
