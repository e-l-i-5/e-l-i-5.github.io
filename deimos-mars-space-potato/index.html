How to Escape a Moon's Gravity on a Bicycle!

You could escape this moon's gravity riding a regular bicycle at just 20 km/h. Welcome to Deimos — Mars' tiny, outer, potato-shaped moon, the forgotten little buddy of the Red Planet and possibly the key to humanity's future there.

First, the scale. Deimos is only about 12.5 km wide — a lumpy, bumpy space potato. Put Earth's Moon, Phobos and Deimos side by side and the comparison is almost comical: our Moon is a world, Phobos is a boulder field, and Deimos is a small hill floating in the dark. Gravity on something that size is barely there, which is why the escape velocity is so ridiculously low: pedal a bicycle at 20 km/h and you could literally jump-ship into space.

Where did it come from? Three origin theories competed — captured asteroid, debris, something else — and the mystery stood for generations. Then, in 2023, space probes cracked it: the Hope Probe's observations showed Deimos is made of real Mars-like rock, not captured asteroid material. The forgotten potato turns out to be family.

From the Martian surface, Deimos barely performs as a moon. It looks like a bright star drifting overhead, so small that it does not eclipse the Sun like a true solar eclipse — instead it transits across the Sun's face as a tiny dot, captured on photos by the Spirit and Opportunity rovers. And it is in no hurry: Deimos takes 2.5 days to set in the west, hanging in the Martian sky like a patient, star-like companion.

Humanity agrees, and the visits are scheduled: from Asaph Hall's discovery in 1877 to JAXA's MMX mission heading there in 2026, the little moon has gone from telescope curiosity to destination. The wildest plan is "sand-braking" — using Deimos and its dusty surface as a staging point, letting future astronauts brake and prepare before dropping onto Mars itself. The forgotten space potato may end up as the stepping stone of an interplanetary species.

FAQ

How weak is Deimos' gravity?

Absurdly weak: the moon is only about 12.5 km wide, and its escape velocity is so low that a cyclist pedaling at 20 km/h could, in principle, launch off the surface into space.

Where did Deimos come from?

For years three origin theories competed. In 2023, the Hope Probe's observations settled the mystery: Deimos is composed of Mars-like rock, meaning it formed from the Red Planet's own material rather than being a captured asteroid.

What does Deimos look like from Mars?

Like a bright, slow star. It is too small to eclipse the Sun — it transits as a tiny dot, photographed by the Spirit and Opportunity rovers — and takes about 2.5 days to set in the west.

Why would astronauts visit Deimos?

As a staging point. The "sand-braking" concept would use the moon's dusty surface to slow and prepare spacecraft before descending to Mars — making Deimos a possible key to humanity's future on the Red Planet.
