Conjunctions: The Superglue Crew of Human Language

Dump a giant box of LEGO blocks on the floor and you get a messy pile of plastic. Words behave the same way: without linguistic superglue, every sentence sounds like a robot reading a shopping list. Dog. Bark. Loud. Run. The glue exists, and it is the humblest part of speech in the box: conjunctions, the tiny connectors the video calls the ultimate bridge builders of human thought. Drop one into I fed the cat. I brushed my clothes, and the pile becomes a thought: After I fed the cat, I brushed my clothes.

The famous crew runs under the acronym FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So, the 7 superstar coordinating connectors. Each holds one of 7 construction jobs: And stacks, But contrasts, Or offers a choice, So delivers the consequence. The video even busts a school myth along the way: the 3rd-grade rule that a sentence may never start with And or But was always false, and The Economist, The Atlantic and Supreme Court rulings break it all the time, because the connectors carry the flow wherever the flow needs to start.

Then comes the buddy system: correlative conjunctions, the pairs that refuse to go anywhere alone. Neither/Nor, Both/And and Whether/Or work as brackets, clamping both halves of a comparison so the sentence gets rhythm and balance. The video's impact test shows the payoff: Both far and fast lands with a beat that a loose rewrite never matches, because the bracketed pair promises a twin and delivers a twin.

The bossy family does the opposite work. Subordinating conjunctions like because, although and long before strip a clause of its independence and anchor it to another, which is how language attaches causes, concessions and, above all, time. Long before the meowing cat test appears, the bossy words have already done their job: they tell you which action is the stage and which is the backdrop, who did what because of what, and how far back the story begins.

Three families, one job: superglue. Coordinators join equals, correlatives bracket pairs, subordinators chain the dependent to the main. Everything else in grammar arranges furniture; these seven-letter crews and bossy timers are what keep the pile of plastic blocks from staying a pile, which is why the video's closing question lands: what bridges are connecting your thoughts today?

FAQ

What do conjunctions do?

They are the superglue between words, clauses and ideas: connectors that turn piles of blocks like Dog. Bark. Loud. Run. into flowing thoughts. The video frames them as the bridge builders of human thought, joining chunks so sentences can carry meaning.

What is FANBOYS?

The acronym for the 7 coordinating connectors: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. Each has its own construction job, from stacking with And to delivering consequences with So, and together they join equal blocks of language.

What are correlative conjunctions?

The buddy system: pairs such as Neither/Nor, Both/And and Whether/Or that never appear alone. They bracket both halves of a comparison, giving sentences balance and rhythm, as in the video's Both far and fast impact test.

Why does the video call some conjunctions bossy?

Subordinators like because, although and long before strip away a clause's independence, anchoring one action to another in cause, concession or time. They decide which clause is the stage and which is the backdrop, which is why the video calls them bossy.
