The Explainer: Vitamin D

Nutrition & biochemistry, explained like you are five.

Every autumn, somewhere on Earth, the sun drops low in the sky — and millions of people quietly begin a shortage they will not notice for months. The missing thing is vitamin D, and it is the strangest nutrient in your pantry: half vitamin, half hormone, and the only one your body can manufacture from plain sunlight.

Start with the manufacture, because it reads like science fiction. Ultraviolet B rays land on your skin and strike a cholesterol-like molecule sitting there, and the impact snaps the molecule into a new shape — pre-vitamin D. Your liver and kidneys then process it twice more, like workshops adding finishing touches, until the final product appears: calcitriol, the active form. So yes — with enough midday sun, you are a solar-powered factory. The same reaction is why vitamin D was never truly a "vitamin" in the classic sense: vitamins, by definition, come from food. This one your skin makes.

What does the finished product do? Its headline job is calcium management. Vitamin D tells your gut to absorb the calcium you eat and helps keep blood levels steady, because your skeleton is not a dead frame — it is a living bank that deposits and withdraws minerals all day. Without the vitamin, the calcium passes through you unused, and the body starts funding itself by borrowing from the bones. In children that loan shows up as rickets, the bent-leg disease that haunted cities before the 20th century; in adults it appears as softer bones, aches, and slow-repairing muscles.

The catch is geography and lifestyle. The same UVB rays that build vitamin D also burn skin, and they only work when the sun is high enough — in winter, far from the equator, the angle is simply wrong, and the factory shuts down for months. Indoor lives, sunscreen, and darker skin (which evolved brilliantly for strong sun and manufactures less in weak light) all shrink production further. That is why fatty fish, egg yolks, fortified milk, and mushrooms treated with UV light matter, and why supplements are so often recommended in the dark half of the year.

Vitamin D, in short, is sunlight bottled for cloudy days. Know your latitude, know your limits — and when the sun drops low, deliver what the sky no longer can.

The short version

  • Vitamin D is made in your skin from UVB sunlight, then activated in two more steps by the liver and kidneys — you are a solar-powered factory.
  • Its main job is calcium: it lets your gut absorb the mineral your bones deposit and withdraw all day; without it, bones soften (rickets in children).
  • In winter, far from the equator, the sun is too low for production — fatty fish, fortified foods, and supplements cover the dark months.

FAQ

Why is vitamin D called the "sunshine vitamin"?

Because your skin makes it directly from UVB light acting on a cholesterol-like molecule. No plant or animal makes it for you the way they make other vitamins — sunlight is the primary source, food is the backup.

What does vitamin D do with calcium?

It acts like a hormone that tells your intestines to absorb dietary calcium and keeps blood levels steady. Bones constantly deposit and withdraw calcium, and vitamin D is what keeps that bank funded.

Why is deficiency worse in winter?

UVB rays only reach the ground when the sun is sufficiently high. In winter at higher latitudes the angle is too low, skin production drops to near zero, and stores run down over the dark months — hence seasonal deficiency.

Can you get too much from the sun?

Sunlight self-regulates: prolonged exposure degrades the precursor in the skin, so the sun alone does not cause overdose. Overdosing happens with supplements, which bypass the built-in limit — stick to recommended doses.
