How Medieval Siblings Accidentally Broke an Entire Empire!

What happens when royal brothers and sisters refuse to share a giant inheritance? They slice the cake into pieces until the whole kingdom shatters. Between 1240 and 1462, medieval Russia lived through a chaotic two-hundred-year crisis that historians call "Appanage Russia". Instead of leaving the realm to one ruler, princes divided their lands among all their sons — and with every generation the slices got smaller and weaker, like a birthday cake cut among too many cousins.

Powerful lands paid the highest price. A mighty realm like Vladimir was split into dozens of tiny, helpless micro-states that could barely defend their own borders. Into this power vacuum marched the Mongol Golden Horde of Batu Khan, and it set three brutal rules for the broken house: every prince needed a yarlik — a permission slip from the Khan — just to rule his own town; everyone paid tribute, the dan, like school lunch money; and fighting back was simply not an option. The bill was staggering: tribute collectors hauled away 15.6 tons of silver.

Yet one clever city played the longest game in the room. Moscow started the climb under Ivan I: it collected the taxes for the Horde, and in 1325 it pulled off a masterstroke by gathering the Church — the one institution that touched every Russian town — and moving its home to Moscow. Cash and faith now flowed to the same address. By the time Ivan the Great took over in 1462, the tables had turned: he spent his reign erasing local rule, gluing the puzzle pieces together permanently, and building absolute power.

The final thought is the heaviest one: three centuries of being bullied by the Horde, sliced apart by inheritance, and robbed of silver forged a lasting instinct — never again leave power divided. That 300-year trauma is why modern centralized power looks the way it does. The cake fight ended, but the winner kept the knife.

FAQ

What were the three Mongol rules?

First, every prince needed a yarlik — the Khan's permission slip — to rule his own town. Second, everyone paid the dan, a tribute so heavy it totaled 15.6 tons of silver. Third, fighting back was off the table, so the fractured states had no way out.

Why did the Church move to Moscow in 1325?

Because Moscow was playing the long game under Ivan I. Gathering the Church meant faith, prestige and money flowed to one city, giving Moscow an advantage no military victory could match — cash and belief now pointed at the same address.

How did the crisis forge modern centralized power?

Three hundred years of being sliced apart and bullied taught the surviving state one lesson: divided power means defeat. When Ivan the Great erased local rule and gathered the lands after 1462, absolute centralized power became the permanent answer to that trauma.
