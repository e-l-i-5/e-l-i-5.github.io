The Nazi Camp Prisoner Who Invented a Superhuman Math Hack

Picture the worst possible laboratory: a Nazi concentration camp, no tools, no paper, no way to write anything down. Now picture a prisoner in there building a whole system of mathematics — in his head. That is the true story of Jakov Trachtenberg, who spent his captivity in a concentration camp during World War II developing, entirely without pen or paper, what became the Speed System of Basic Mathematics. Most people's deepest thinking needs a scratchpad; Trachtenberg had to become his own scratchpad.

The system he emerged with does something almost rude to the times tables: it eliminates them completely. No more memorizing the grid up to twelve — the Trachtenberg method replaces messy multiplication with a handful of simple addition rules, all built on one core secret the video calls the Neighbor Concept: in Trachtenberg arithmetic, you never really multiply a number; you work digit by digit, and each digit's answer leans on its neighbor beside it.

Watch the trick at work. The Rule of 11 claims you can multiply 3,425 × 11 in your head in 3 seconds — and you can, because multiplying by 11 is just adding neighbors: the answer's digits are running sums of each digit and the one next to it, and 3,425 × 11 lands on 37,675 without a single memorized product. The Rule of 12 says: double each digit and add the neighbor. Take the video's worked example, 316 × 12: start at the right — double 6, add the (empty) neighbor, get 12, write 2 and carry 1; double 1, add neighbor 6, add the carry — 9; double 3, add neighbor 1 — 7; finally double the invisible leading zero, add neighbor 3 — 3. Read it off: 3,792. Exactly right, no times table consulted.

And for the friendliest multiplier of all, 5, the system offers the Halving Trick — featured in the movie "Gifted" — turning multiplication by 5 into something closer to halving than to multiplying. Giant numbers fall in seconds, and the calculator stays in your pocket.

There is a quiet lesson packed inside the hack. Trachtenberg's method is proof that constraints do not have to be the end of thinking — sometimes they are the forge. Deprived of paper, he rewrote the mechanics of arithmetic to fit entirely inside a human head, and the result was not a weaker version of school math but a cleaner one: fewer steps, less memory, more structure. A system built for survival under the cruelest conditions now survives as a gift to anyone who has ever stared at a multiplication problem and wished it would just solve itself.

FAQ

Who was Jakov Trachtenberg?

A prisoner in a Nazi concentration camp during World War II who, entirely in his head and without pen or paper, created the Speed System of Basic Mathematics — a complete mental arithmetic method born from the harshest possible conditions.

What is the "Neighbor Concept"?

The core secret of the Trachtenberg system: instead of recalling multiplication tables, you compute digit by digit, and each digit's result is built by adding its neighbor — turning messy multiplication into simple addition rules.

How does the Rule of 12 work?

Double each digit and add the neighbor. For 316 × 12: double the rightmost 6 (12, write 2, carry 1), double 1 and add neighbor 6 plus carry (9), double 3 and add neighbor 1 (7), then 0 doubled plus neighbor 3 gives 3 — reading back: 3,792.

Where did the Halving Trick appear outside math?

In the movie "Gifted," which features multiplying by 5 through halving. The trick turns the friendliest multiplier into near-halving, which is why Trachtenberg math can crush giant numbers in seconds.
