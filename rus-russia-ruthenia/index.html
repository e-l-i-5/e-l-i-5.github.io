How 1 Name Got Split into Russia, Ruthenia, and Rus'

Here is a riddle hiding in every modern atlas: Russia, Ruthenia, and Rus' look like three different places, but they are the exact same name wearing three different language outfits. A person named William gets called Will by friends, Bill by coworkers, and Guillermo by his grandmother — and a thousand years ago, history played that same game of broken telephone with one medieval word, splitting it across the map of Eastern Europe.

The story starts with rowers. Around a thousand years ago, traveling Vikings — the men who rowed their longships down the great rivers of the East — were known by an Old Norse root meaning roughly "the men who row": rods-. From that root came the name of the state they rowed to and eventually ruled: Rus'. A country named after rowing — the most practical nickname in history — and it stuck, appearing in chronicles as the name of the great medieval kingdom centered on the river trade.

Then the kingdom fractured, and its neighbors picked up the name like relatives dividing an inheritance. Each chancellery wrote the word in its own alphabet, with its own accent. The Byzantine Greeks — who traded with the kingdom and baptized it — gave the name their grand imperial twist, turning Rus' into "Rhōssía," the form that flows directly into today's Russia. Western European scribes, writing in Latin, heard and rendered the same word as "Ruthenia" — the Latin gown that decorated half the maps of medieval and early modern Europe. One single word, two different roads: the Greek road became the name of the empire, the Latin road became the scholarly label for its lands.

You can still see the split frozen in antique cartography. The 1549 map of Sigismund von Herberstein — an ambassador who actually traveled there — labels the territory with Latin-built names, showing "Ruthenia" where Greek-influenced tradition would later say "Russia." Neither mapmaker was confused; they were each reporting the name through the language outfit their readers wore. The map is a photograph of the telephone game mid-call.

What makes the story remarkable is how little changed underneath. Strip away the Greek grandeur and the Latin gowns, and every form — Rus', Rhōssía, Russia, Ruthenia — carries the same three letters at heart, pointing back to those Viking rowers pushing against the current. Names usually change because places change; here, the place stayed put and the spellings drifted, each neighbor filing the word under its own grammar.

So the next time a world map shows Russia and an old chronicle mentions Ruthenia, you can quietly enjoy the secret: they are the same word that started on a river, shouted by rowers, and echoed for a thousand years through Greek courts and Latin scriptoria until the game of continental telephone gave one name three costumes.

FAQ

Where does the name Rus' come from?

From the Old Norse root rods-, meaning "the men who row." Viking travelers rowed down Eastern Europe's rivers for trade and conquest, and the state that formed around them took their rowing nickname as its name.

Are Russia and Ruthenia different countries?

Historically the words referred to the same medieval land — Rus' — just filtered through different languages. "Russia" descends from the Byzantine Greek form (Rhōssía), while "Ruthenia" is the Latin version used by Western scribes and mapmakers.

Why do old Western maps say Ruthenia?

Western European cartographers wrote in Latin, so they recorded the name in its Latin gown. The 1549 map of Sigismund von Herberstein, for example, uses Latin-built names — showing how the same word reached Western readers through a different linguistic path.

Did the meaning of the name change over time?

The core stayed remarkably stable — all forms point back to the rowers — but the spelling drifted with each neighbor's alphabet and accent. The kingdom's fracture meant no single authority controlled the spelling, so the Greek form grew into "Russia" while the Latin form became "Ruthenia."
