The Traffic Cop Your Diet Forgot to Hire

Nutrition & biochemistry, explained like you are five.

Picture calcium as a fleet of cars driving through your body. The roads are busy, the cargo is essential, but without a director at the intersections, cars end up parked in all the wrong places, and a traffic jam in an artery is nothing like a parking spot in a bone. The video's hero is the director: Vitamin K2, the unsung cousin of vitamins C and D, a specialized traffic cop working 24/7 to wave calcium into bones and teeth while holding up a STOP sign at the heart and arteries.

The personnel are already on the payroll, and the video names them briefly: Osteocalcin, the builder who escorts calcium into bone, and MGP, the bouncer who keeps it out of blood vessel walls. The workers exist in everyone. What the video calls the 90% problem is the cop: most diets never deliver Vitamin K2 at all, so the builder stands idle and the bouncer works short-staffed, while calcium circulates without directions. Having the machinery is not the same as having the manager.

The recruiting problem starts with a mix-up between two cousins. Vitamin K1, the clotting vitamin, lives in your salad, and its job is your blood, not your bones. Vitamin K2, menaquinone, is the calcium manager, and it comes from different shelves entirely: your cheese helps your bones where your salad helps your blood. The video's golden rule is not to confuse them, because a diet can be perfectly green and still be missing the cop.

So where do you find K2? The video hands out a scouting report of superstars. Natto, the Japanese fermented soybean dish, is the outright champion, with goose liver and grass-fed butter behind it, plus everyday supports like eggs and Gouda cheese. It also distinguishes the two main forms, MK-4 and MK-7, which is a reminder that even inside the K2 family there are variants with different resumes.

The quiet conclusion is that calcium management is a hiring problem before it is a supplement problem. The city has roads, destinations, builders and bouncers; what 90% of diets lack is the one employee who tells traffic where to go. Hire the cop, from natto upward, and the same calcium that once idled in the wrong neighborhoods starts reporting to the bones where it was always meant to work.

FAQ

What does Vitamin K2 do in the body?

Per the video, K2 directs calcium traffic: it helps route the mineral into bones and teeth while putting up stop signs that keep it out of the heart and arteries. The worker proteins Osteocalcin and MGP carry out the moves, with K2 as the director.

How is K2 different from K1?

K1 is the clotting vitamin found in greens, and it serves your blood. K2, menaquinone, is the calcium manager found in foods like cheese. The video's golden rule is not to mix them up: a salad-heavy diet can still lack K2.

Which foods are the best sources of K2?

The video ranks natto, Japanese fermented soybeans, as the champion, with goose liver and grass-fed butter as superstars, and eggs and Gouda cheese as everyday sources. It also names the two main forms, MK-4 and MK-7.

Why does the video talk about a 90% problem?

Because most diets, it says, are missing the traffic cop entirely: roughly 90% of people may not get meaningful K2. The workers and the calcium are present, but without the director the mineral lacks directions to the right neighborhoods.
