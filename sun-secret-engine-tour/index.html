The Secret Engine Inside the Sun

You look at it every day, but the Sun is hiding a machinery so strange that each part of it sounds made up. Start with the scale: you could stuff 1.3 million Earths into its belly, and it still holds 99.86 percent of the entire Solar System's mass -- every planet, moon and asteroid combined is a rounding error. The recipe is almost embarrassing in its simplicity: hydrogen and helium, the two lightest ingredients in the universe.

The engine itself runs in three floors. In the core, nuclear fusion smashes hydrogen atoms together like LEGO bricks, welding them into helium and releasing the energy that lights everything you have ever seen. Above it sits the radiative zone, where that energy crawls outward, and then the churning convection zone, where hot plasma rises, cools and sinks like water in a boiling pot. And here is the paradox that breaks intuition: the Sun is a compost pile of sorts -- per cubic meter, its core produces less power than a heap of decomposing grass. It wins by sheer size, not intensity.

Light pays for the escape dearly. A photon born in the core cannot simply fly out; it ricochets through the dense interior for about 170,000 years before reaching the photosphere, the glowing surface where light finally breaks free. The warmth landing on your face today is, in a real sense, 170,000 years old.

Stranger still is what floats above it. The corona -- the Sun's outer atmosphere -- burns at millions of degrees, hundreds of times hotter than the surface below it, and nobody expected that: it is as if the air above a candle were hotter than the flame. Tangled magnetic fields twist through sunspots, snap in solar flares and coronal mass ejections, and paint the Northern Lights across our sky when the debris arrives.

The engine has an ending too. One day the Sun will swell into a red giant, swallowing the inner planets' orbit, then shed its outer layers and settle down as a small white dwarf -- a dense ember cooling for eternity. Every sunrise is a machine mid-performance, running from hydrogen brick to final ember.

The short version

  • The Sun holds 99.86% of the Solar System's mass -- 1.3 million Earths would fit inside -- and runs on the simplest recipe: hydrogen fused into helium in its core.
  • A photon needs about 170,000 years to ricochet out of the dense interior to the photosphere, so the warmth on your face is 170,000-year-old heat.
  • The corona burns at millions of degrees -- hotter than the surface below -- and tangled magnetic fields drive sunspots, flares and the Northern Lights; eventually the Sun becomes a red giant, then a white dwarf.

FAQ

What is inside the Sun?

Three floors: the core, where nuclear fusion smashes hydrogen atoms into helium like LEGO bricks; the radiative zone, where energy crawls outward; and the convection zone, where hot plasma rises and sinks like boiling water. Above them sits the photosphere, the surface where light finally escapes.

How old is the sunlight that reaches Earth?

About 170,000 years older than you think. A photon born in the core ricochets through the dense interior for roughly 170,000 years before breaking free at the photosphere -- so the heat on your face left the Sun's core long before human civilization existed.

Why is the corona hotter than the Sun's surface?

That is the coronal heating paradox: the outer atmosphere burns at millions of degrees, hundreds of times hotter than the photosphere beneath it -- as if the air above a candle were hotter than the flame. The energy is fed by the Sun's tangled magnetic fields, the same forces that snap into flares and coronal mass ejections.
