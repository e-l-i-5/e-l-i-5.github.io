The Planet That Turned Into Literal Hell

Astronomy, explained like you are five.

Look up after sunset and you may spot it: the brightest "star" in the sky, calm and beautiful. That is not a star. It is Venus, Earth's closest planetary neighbor — and beneath those dazzling clouds sits the closest thing to literal hell in our Solar System.

The nightmare starts with the size. Venus is almost a perfect twin of Earth: nearly the same size, nearly the same mass, built from the same ingredients. For a moment in the early Solar System, two worlds ran the same experiment. Then Venus's copy went catastrophically wrong. Its atmosphere is 96% carbon dioxide — a heat-trapping blanket thousands of times denser than anything on Earth — and its clouds are not made of water. They are made of sulfuric acid.

The result is a runaway greenhouse effect. Sunlight gets in, but the CO2 blanket will not let the heat back out. The surface temperature climbed until it settled at about 464°C — hot enough to melt lead, and hotter than Mercury, a planet that hugs the Sun itself. Standing on Venus, you would also feel the pressure: 92 times heavier than Earth's, the equivalent of swimming half a mile deep in the ocean. Landers that reached the surface survived barely an hour or two before the heat and pressure crushed them.

We know the terrain anyway, because radar sees through the clouds. It revealed a world paved in lava: about 85,000 volcanoes — more than any other planet we know — vast molten fields, and alien landforms with names to match, like arachnoids (spider-shaped volcano webs) and pancake domes. The winds up in the clouds scream around the planet at 300 km/h, so fast the whole atmosphere super-rotates around Venus in just a few Earth days.

And then there is the calendar joke. Venus spins backwards compared to almost everything else, so the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east. It spins so slowly that a single Venusian day lasts longer than its entire year. Ancient astronomers knew Venus as two different objects — the Evening Star and the Morning Star — before realizing both were the same cosmic mirror.

Venus is not just a curiosity. Carl Sagan warned that it is our own story's cautionary tale: Earth's twin, destroyed by a greenhouse effect that started small. Venus shows what a climate can do when the blanket gets too thick — a lesson delivered by the brightest light in our evening sky.

The short version

  • Venus is Earth's near-twin in size, but a runaway greenhouse effect turned it into a 464°C pressure cooker with 96% CO2 air and clouds of sulfuric acid.
  • The surface hides 85,000 volcanoes, crushing pressure 92 times Earth's, backwards sunrises in the west, and a day that lasts longer than a year.
  • Carl Sagan called it Earth's warning: the twin planet shows what a thickening greenhouse blanket can do to a world.

FAQ

Why is Venus hotter than Mercury if Mercury is closer to the Sun?

Mercury has almost no atmosphere to hold heat, so it cools on its night side. Venus's dense CO2 blanket traps heat day and night across the whole planet, pushing its surface to 464°C — hotter than any other planet.

What are Venus's clouds made of?

Not water. They are droplets and crystals of sulfuric acid, formed from volcanic gases in a thick, dry atmosphere. Below the clouds, the air is almost entirely carbon dioxide.

How do we know what the surface looks like?

Clouds block visible light, so spacecraft map Venus with radar, which pierces the clouds. Radar has revealed lava plains, huge volcanoes, and strange landforms like arachnoids and pancake domes.

What does Venus teach us about Earth?

It shows the endpoint of a runaway greenhouse effect. Earth and Venus started as twins; Venus's blanket of CO2 locked its heat in. Keeping Earth's own greenhouse gases in balance is the lesson the brightest evening star writes across the sky.
