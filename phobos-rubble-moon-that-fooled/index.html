The Secret Truth About Phobos: Mars' Doomed Space Potato

Of all the moons in the Solar System, none orbits its planet closer than Phobos circles Mars -- a lumpy, potato-shaped world zipping around the red planet in just 7 hours and 39 minutes. That is faster than Mars itself spins, which is why Phobos does something almost no moon can: it rises in the west and sets in the east, crossing the Martian sky backwards twice a day. Up close, the "moon" gets stranger still. Phobos is not a solid object at all. It is a porous rubble pile -- a loose bag of gravel barely holding its own shape -- so weakly bound that, the video notes, you could throw a baseball into orbit around it.

The little space potato has a resume of near-catastrophes. Its face carries the Stickney crater, a 9-kilometer scar from an impact that nearly shattered the moon outright -- whatever survived that hit would be shaken to pieces by almost anything else. Streaking away from the crater run grooves up to 20 kilometers long: cosmic stretch marks, cracks drawn across the surface where the battered body is literally being pulled apart by the planet it circles.

Phobos even fooled the experts. In the 1960s, its strange orbit inspired a serious-sounding theory that the moon was hollow -- an artificial space station launched by someone else -- and top scientists took the idea seriously enough to debate it. The truth turned out to be more humbling: a natural rubble pile, light and porous enough to wobble oddly, pretending to be a spaceship. The mystery did not end there, though. Where Phobos came from is still an open fight between three main theories -- with one camp reading red Martian dust in its skin and another pointing to the dark, asteroid-like blue rocks beneath -- so the moon keeps one foot in fact and one in open questions.

And the whole story has a deadline. Phobos is locked in a slow-motion death spiral, losing about 2 centimeters of altitude every year as tidal gravity drags it down. The countdown runs roughly 43 million years, at the end of which Mars' gravity will tear the rubble pile apart and spread it into a magnificent planetary ring. A moon that rises backwards, wears near-death scars, once masqueraded as an alien station, and is scheduled to become a halo -- the Solar System rarely does tragedy this theatrical.

FAQ

Why does Phobos rise in the west?

Because it orbits Mars in 7 hours 39 minutes -- faster than Mars rotates. From the ground, the moon overtakes the planet's spin, so it appears to cross the sky backwards: rising in the west and setting in the east.

What is the hollow space station story about Phobos?

In the 1960s, Phobos' strange orbit led some scientists to seriously propose that the moon was hollow and artificial -- a space station. The porous rubble-pile reality explained the odd behavior naturally, and the alien-station theory collapsed.

What will happen to Phobos in the end?

It is spiraling down about 2 centimeters per year. In roughly 43 million years, Mars' gravity will exceed the rubble pile's own strength and tear it into debris that spreads into a planetary ring.
